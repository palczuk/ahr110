Heat, Temperature, and Pressure

Summary by topic · 1.1–1.13

1.1

Temperature

Definition: Measures the level or intensity of heat, not the total heat content.

Cold: The absence of heat.

Standard boiling/freezing points: water freezes at 32°F (0°C) and boils at 212°F (100°C) at sea-level atmospheric pressure.

Absolute zero: -460°F (-273°C), the theoretical point where all molecular motion ceases and no heat remains.

Temperature scales: Fahrenheit (°F), Celsius (°C), Rankine (°R), and Kelvin (K).

1.2

Temperature Conversion Formulas

Celsius to Fahrenheit: °F = (1.8 × °C) + 32

Fahrenheit to Celsius: °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8

Absolute temperature: Rankine = °F + 460; Kelvin = °C + 273.

1.3

Introduction to Heat

Heat is a form of energy that cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed (First Law of Thermodynamics).

Heat naturally flows from a warmer substance to a cooler substance.

British Thermal Unit (Btu): heat required to raise 1 pound of water by 1°F.

1.4

Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Conduction: heat transfer from molecule to molecule through direct physical contact.

Convection: heat transfer via a moving fluid medium (liquid or gas), natural or forced.

Radiation: heat transfer through electromagnetic waves traveling through space.

1.5

Sensible Heat

Heat added or removed that causes a temperature change without changing the state of matter.

Easily measured using a standard thermometer.

1.6

Latent Heat

Heat added or removed that causes a change of state without any change in temperature (“hidden heat”).

Latent heat of vaporization: 970 Btu/lb for water at 212°F.

Latent heat of condensation: heat removed to change vapor back to liquid.

Latent heat of fusion: 144 Btu/lb for ice/water at 32°F.

1.7

Specific Heat

Number of Btus needed to raise 1 pound of a substance by 1°F.

Water has a specific heat of 1.0 Btu/lb/°F.

Sensible heat formula: Q = W × c × ΔT.

1.8

Total Heat (Enthalpy)

Total heat content contained in a substance, combining sensible heat and latent heat.

Used in HVACR to calculate required system capacity for cooling and dehumidification.

1.9

Atmospheric Pressure

Sea-level standard: 14.696 psia or 29.92 in. Hg at standard atmospheric conditions.

Atmospheric pressure decreases with higher altitude, lowering the boiling point of liquids.

1.10

Gauge Pressure (psig) vs. Absolute Pressure (psia)

psig (Gauge Pressure): reads zero under normal atmospheric pressure.

psia (Absolute Pressure): includes atmospheric pressure.

Conversions: psia = psig + 14.7; psig = psia − 14.7.

1.11

Vacuum and Inches of Mercury (in. Hg)

Pressures below atmospheric are measured in inches of mercury (in. Hg) or microns for deep vacuums.

Formula: psia = (30 − vacuum in. Hg) ÷ 2.

1.12

Pressure-Temperature Relationship

Under a constant pressure, a liquid boils at a specific temperature.

Increasing pressure raises the boiling point; lowering pressure lowers the boiling point.

1.13

HVACR System Pressure Measuring Instruments

Bourdon Tube Gauge: mechanical gauge utilizing a curved brass tube that straightens under pressure.

Compound Gauge: measures pressure (psig) above atmospheric and vacuum (in. Hg) below atmospheric.

Digital Gauges & Micron Gauges: high-precision instruments used to measure deep vacuums during system evacuation.
